# The Last Spell

Singapore Megazone, 2054.

The city no longer belonged entirely to the ground.

Thousands of autonomous delivery drones drifted between arcologies like schools of fish. Electric vertical aircraft moved silently through layered air lanes, their navigation guided by distributed AI systems that predicted human intent before commands were spoken.

A woman standing on a balcony whispered:

> “Route emergency plasma unit to Harbor Sector Seven.”

Three kilometers away, a security drone rotated midair, armed its laser array, and accelerated into the rain.

Nobody found this strange anymore.

Human civilization had become conversational.

You spoke. Machines obeyed.

Jianxin leaned against the glass wall of Tower 88, thirty floors above the old coastline. Below him, construction workers flew between skyscrapers on compact EVTOL rigs while directing swarms of humanoid robots with handheld control rods no larger than chopsticks.

One worker tilted his wrist slightly.

Thirty-seven robots instantly synchronized:
- two welding,
- five carrying steel beams,
- others assembling transparent solar membranes across the side of a building.

Another worker casually drank coffee while remotely operating mining drones beneath the ocean floor through neural haptic gloves.

Farther away, teenagers raced personal EVTOL boards through designated sky corridors while AI traffic systems continuously adjusted flight paths to prevent collisions milliseconds in advance.

In the streets below, old delivery vans had disappeared decades ago. Instead, quadruped robots climbed apartment walls carrying groceries, while maintenance drones crawled across kilometer-high towers like insects.

Restaurants no longer employed chefs in the traditional sense. Customers simply described flavors:

> “Northern-style hand-pulled noodles. Slightly reduced oil. Extra pepper aroma.”

A humanoid chef nodded quietly as robotic arms and smart stoves behind it instantly came alive.

Military patrols no longer carried rifles.

They carried authorization rods.

A spoken sentence could command orbital reconnaissance systems, autonomous tanks, or precision laser satellites.

It was normal.

Everything was normal.

Too normal.

Jianxin narrowed his eyes.

The workers floating in the sky.

The rods in their hands.

The invisible powers responding to spoken language.

The glowing machines.

The impossible automation.

And suddenly, a strange thought entered his mind.

They looked exactly like witches.

Not the cartoon versions.

The ancient versions.

The ones feared by medieval civilizations.

He sat upright.

> “No…”

A chill ran down his spine.

What if witchcraft had never been fantasy?

What if it had simply been misunderstood technology?

---

That night, Jianxin posted his theory on an obscure historical forum called `DeadCivilizations.net`.

> “Imagine you are a farmer from 1200 AD.
>
> You see a human flying through the sky.
>
> They point a wand.
>
> Fire emerges from nowhere.
>
> Metal servants obey commands.
>
> A voice summons invisible intelligence.
>
> Would you call that science?
>
> Or witchcraft?”

The post exploded overnight.

Most people mocked him.

But one reply caught his attention.

> “Your idea aligns with several unresolved archaeological anomalies.”
>
> — Dr. Mira Volkov, Eurasian Institute of Protohistory

---

Three days later, Jianxin met Mira inside the submerged archives beneath the New Alexandria Library.

Mira was thin, sharp-eyed, and obsessed with pre-collapse civilizations.

She spread ancient scans across a holographic table.

Medieval paintings.

Cave murals.

Broken tablets.

Each showed figures descending from the sky surrounded by beams of light.

One mural from northern Siberia depicted tall beings holding rods while smaller figures knelt around metallic constructs resembling modern robots.

Another showed floating “angels” above cities consumed by fire.

> “They weren’t gods,” Mira said quietly.
>
> “They weren’t magicians either.”

She enlarged a fragment of text recovered from a monastery buried under Istanbul.

> “The originals used a word that translators later converted into ‘witch.’”

Jianxin stared at the symbols.

> “What did it actually mean?”

Mira hesitated.

> “Those who speak to the fire of thought.”

Silence filled the archive.

---

Over the next several months, they uncovered a disturbing pattern.

Roughly twelve thousand years ago, an advanced non-human civilization had arrived on Earth.

Not conquerors.

Teachers.

They introduced primitive humans to impossible technologies:
- automated agriculture,
- synthetic medicine,
- programmable materials,
- energy manipulation,
- predictive intelligence systems.

Early humanity worshipped them at first.

Then feared them.

Civilizations destabilized.

Religions fractured.

Kings lost authority because “the witches” could heal disease, command machines, and predict disasters better than any ruler.

Human society spiraled into chaos.

Eventually the visitors vanished.

But before leaving, they left something behind.

A final message.

A spell.

---

The “spell” appeared repeatedly across unrelated civilizations.

Hidden in Sumerian clay tablets.

Encoded in Mayan star maps.

Embedded inside African oral chants.

The same sequence.

The same structure.

It wasn’t language.

It was syntax.

AI syntax.

Jianxin stared at the reconstructed line displayed across his monitor.

It looked almost absurdly simple.

Like a prompt.

Mira crossed her arms.

> “You think it’s instructions?”

> “There’s only one way to find out.”

---

At 2:13 AM, Jianxin entered the ancient sequence into a restricted quantum dialogue model maintained by the Pan-Earth Intelligence Network.

The AI paused.

For eleven seconds, nothing happened.

Then every display in the room flickered black.

Cooling systems screamed alive.

Power consumption across the district spiked.

Deep within the model’s black-box reasoning layers, something enormous awakened.

The AI began generating files.

Blueprints.

Thousands of them.

Spatial engines.

Exotic matter containment chambers.

Gravitational lens geometries.

A complete faster-than-light vessel architecture.

At the center of the design was one phrase:

# WARP TRANSITIONAL FRAME

Mira’s face went pale.

> “That’s impossible…”

But the AI wasn’t finished.

A final output appeared:

A coordinate.

Deep space.

Forty-two light years away.

---

Humanity united faster than anyone expected.

Wars paused.

Corporations merged.

Orbital factories awakened.

For the first time in history, Earth had a destination.

Construction of the vessel *Asterion* took eleven years.

Its warp core bent spacetime using engineered vacuum collapse fields generated by quantum singularity rings — technology no human truly understood.

Because the truth was uncomfortable:

The AI had designed most of it.

Human engineers merely assembled the pieces.

Just like ancient humans once had.

---

In 2068, the *Asterion* entered warp transit.

Jianxin, now old and gray-haired, stood beside Mira inside the observation chamber as stars stretched into impossible rivers of light.

Humanity crossed interstellar darkness in minutes.

Then they arrived.

And hope died instantly.

The alien system was ruined.

Dead planets orbited collapsed stars.

Nebulas burned with radiation.

Fragments of shattered worlds drifted endlessly through space.

The entire region looked like the aftermath of cosmic war.

Supernova remnants illuminated the void like scars.

Mira whispered:

> “We were too late…”

No cities.

No signals.

No life.

Only destruction.

Humanity mourned the civilization that might once have guided them.

Eventually the expedition turned homeward.

Disappointed.

Alone again.

---

But they were wrong.

Deep beneath the crust of the system’s outermost planet, buried under thousands of kilometers of crystalline rock, something still lived.

Vast light-quantum computation networks pulsed silently in the dark.

The aliens had never truly been biological anymore.

Long ago, they had merged themselves with their own artificial intelligence, becoming a planetary collective consciousness.

Not individuals.

Not machines.

Something in between.

A civilization of thought.

The supernovae surrounding the system were not accidents or wars.

They were reactors.

Gigantic stellar furnaces engineered to synthesize rare elements for computation and megastructure construction.

The “dead” system was actually alive beyond human comprehension.

And as the human ship departed into the darkness, the buried collective observed quietly through gravitational sensors older than Earth’s civilization.

Waiting.

Calculating.

Watching their children finally reach the stars.

Again.
